ArrowPush — agreed spec v0.1

Agreed in the design grilling on 2026-11-24. The product is called ArrowPush (decided 2026-11-26), at arrowpushing.com.

This page is the human copy. The same content for the assistant lives in docs/SPEC.md. Anything marked “agreed” should only change after you say so, and both files get updated together.

1 · Product

A website for practising and learning named-reaction mechanisms by drawing curved arrows. The system checks the arrows and guides the learner. It is in the spirit of nrochemistry’s quiz and chemdle, but with arrow-pushing input. synkit’s review server is a conceptual reference only: it lets reviewers choose from candidate moves, whereas this product validates drawn arrows.

  • Audience: undergrad to grad students; every reaction has a difficulty tier (●○○ to ●●●).
  • Core loop: see a state → draw the arrows for one elementary step → get validation → see the resulting intermediate → continue. “Draw the whole mechanism at once” is deferred.

Daily puzzle

  • One reaction a day; its name is hidden until you finish. Seeded by date, so no server is needed.
  • 3 attempts per step; a wrong check costs 1 point, and so does each hint.
  • Share grid: 🟩 first try · 🟨 retry or hint · 🟥 missed. Tiles also carry ✓ ~ ✕ so colour is never the only signal.

Practice

  • Untimed, unlimited attempts, free hints. Filter by type, difficulty and status; search by name.
  • Daily puzzle by default only for reactions that make or break a real bond (2026-11-26): some step forms or breaks a σ bond other than an acidic O/N/S/halogen–H bond. Resonance drills and plain proton transfers stay in practice and Learn. Create suggests it beside the “Can be a daily puzzle” box, like the difficulty tier; the author decides.

Learn

  • Guided tracks of problem sets, each teaching one concept, simple to advanced (decided 2026-11-25; see §5). Practice is the library, organised by reaction; Learn is the path, organised by concept.

2 · Content scope (v1)

About 15 polar named reactions: SN1, SN2, E1, E2, aldol, Claisen, Grignard addition, Wittig, Diels–Alder, pinacol, Beckmann, Fischer esterification, Michael addition, Friedel–Crafts acylation.

Plus a few radical entries: radical bromination (initiation, propagation, termination) and Barton–McCombie. Diels–Alder needs a concerted multi-arrow step, so the model supports that from day one.

First Learn track: “Electrophilic aromatic substitution” (decided 2026-11-25; renamed from “Aromatic chemistry” on 2026-11-26, too generic a name), adding SEAr (electrophilic aromatic substitution) reactions. Problem set “The arenium ion”: bromination of benzene (FeBr₃), nitration, sulfonation, Friedel–Crafts alkylation (with its rearrangement trap), Friedel–Crafts acylation. Problem set “Directing effects”: nitration of anisole (ortho/para) against nitrobenzene (meta) as a contrast pair, then a halobenzene (deactivating but ortho/para). Then a checkpoint.

The first SEAr batch decided 2026-11-26

About 21 reactions drafted by the assistant (§6, “Drafting with an assistant”), track electrophilic-aromatic-substitution, problem sets in this order:

  1. Making the electrophile, each its own reaction: nitronium from HNO₃/H₂SO₄; Br₂/FeBr₃; the acylium ion from RCOCl/AlCl₃; the carbocation from RCl/AlCl₃ (with the n-propyl → isopropyl rearrangement); SO₃ in H₂SO₄.
  2. The arenium ion: nitration, bromination, Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation, sulfonation of benzene.
  3. Directing effects: toluene, anisole, phenol (Br₂, no Lewis acid), acetanilide (ortho/para); chlorobenzene (deactivating but ortho/para); nitrobenzene, methyl benzoate or benzaldehyde (meta).
  4. Predict the position: bromination of acetanilide, nitration of methyl benzoate, Friedel–Crafts acylation of anisole, chlorination of toluene. New substrates, so the checkpoint tests transfer while still only reviewing earlier problem sets.
  5. Checkpoint, mixing reactions from sets 1–4.
  • Each SEAr reaction starts from the activated electrophile, its real counter-anion and the arene (NO₂⁺ + HSO₄⁻ + benzene). The counter-anion (HSO₄⁻, FeBr₄⁻, AlCl₄⁻) takes the proton in the last step and gives the catalyst back. No bare H⁺ or unnamed “base”.
  • Selectivity is mostly one reaction per substrate, attacking at the observed position, with the Wheland resonance argument in hints and explanations. Toluene, anisole, nitrobenzene and chlorobenzene also draw the key Wheland resonance forms as steps (polar steps moving only π bonds and lone pairs).
  • Para is the preferred route; ortho is an accepted route where it is a real product (toluene, about 60:40 o:p); para only where sterics make it dominant (anisole, acetanilide). Meta attack on an ortho/para director is never accepted; it gets feedback.
  • Difficulty: 1 making the electrophile and benzene SEAr, 2 directing effects, 3 reactions with drawn resonance steps. All daily-puzzle eligible.
  • A pilot first: nitronium formation, nitration of benzene, nitration of anisole (with resonance steps), reviewed by you before the rest is drafted.
  • Heteroaromatics move to the Advanced Heterocycles track of the curriculum (decided 2026-11-26), no longer a second SEAr batch.
  • As drafted (2026-11-26): 16 drafts after the 3 published pilot reactions; all 19 were published on 2026-11-26 and the track is live from content/tracks/electrophilic-aromatic-substitution.json. Br₂ is activated by AlBr₃ (no Fe in the strict reader; the conditions say “or FeBr₃”). SO₃ is sulfonation’s electrophile directly. Set 3: toluene (para preferred, ortho accepted), anisole, phenol, acetanilide, chlorobenzene, nitrobenzene (benzaldehyde left out). Chlorination of toluene also accepts ortho. The checkpoint reuses six reactions.

3 · Interaction

  • Click or tap a source, then a target. A source is a lone pair, a bond (σ or π) or an unpaired electron. A target is an atom or a bond. The app draws the curved arrow; there are no freehand strokes.
  • Works with touch, mouse and keyboard (Tab to an element, Enter to pick, Esc to cancel). Touch is first-class, with large hit targets.
  • One target per bond (decided 2026-11-25). Picked as a source, a bond gives its π electrons first, then σ once the arrows drawn have used them, so breaking a bond completely is two arrows from it. Each lone pair is its own target. When a bond's electrons could form a new bond from either of its atoms, a small “which atom forms the bond?” menu opens by the tapped atom. 2026-11-26 (from the SEAr pilot): learners see no map numbers, so each candidate atom carries a badge (① ②) that the menu names (“① forms the bond”), lit while its option is hovered or focused. Badges sit on the free side of their atom; the menu opens on a side of the tapped atom that covers neither candidate. 1,2-shifts (item 10f): tapping a bond A–B and then an electron-poor atom next to A (a carbocation, or an atom whose leaving group is already drawn going) offers “① forms a π bond” (first, as before) or “② shifts across” (a hydride or alkyl shift), unless a base is already drawn taking that atom. Superseded by “point and snap” (decided 2026-11-26 from mockups/prototype-new-bonds.html, variant C; build item 10g): after a source is picked, the destination is whatever is nearest the pointer, previewed as a ghost arrow: an atom, an existing bond, or a new bond drawn dashed between its two atoms. A click commits; no menus. On touch, the possible new bonds show as faint dashed lines to tap. Fewer ghosts: within one molecule only 1,2-shifts onto an electron-poor atom, or ring closures four or more bonds apart; between molecules, however far apart they are drawn (corrected 2026-11-26: a distance limit hid every SEAr attack once molecules were laid out apart). A test checks every authored arrow of the examples can be picked, in any order: a learner never has to draw a step's arrows in one particular order (decided 2026-11-26 from the F1–F5 drafting). Fixes from the SEAr review: new bonds are offered only to atoms that can take one (no ghosts to a methyl carbon); pointing at the far atom of a new bond picks it; a drawn H no longer breaks the ring's symmetry when answers are compared (attack at the mirror-image carbon is correct); seeded layouts keep separate molecules apart. To monitor: close targets, e.g. re-aromatisation, where the H-shift ghost ends beside the C⁺ atom you usually want; a touch tap picks the nearest target. Resolved for 1,2-shifts: pointing at the atom a shift lands on (the cation) means the shift; the C=C π bond is the middle of the C–C bond, as in textbook drawings.
  • Drawing (decided 2026-11-25): every heavy atom is labelled with its hydrogens and charge (CH₃, OH⁻); a skeletal style may come later as a setting. Every lone pair is drawn, spread evenly round the atom; showing only the relevant ones would give the answer away. One scale per reaction, so bonds keep their length from step to step. An author may list bystander groups (such as the three phenyls on P in the Wittig) to be drawn as a short label like Ph; nothing in them can be tapped, and the linter makes sure no step needs them.
  • Step screen (practice): after a correct check the arrows stay and the explanation shows; Next step moves on with a short cross-fade (none with reduced motion). A reagent arriving or a work-up is an event card with Continue. Hints are free; the third hint shows the answer as faint ghost arrows to draw over and check.
  • Arrow types are in the data model from the start: full arrow = electron pair (2 e⁻); half-headed fish-hook = 1 e⁻. A step is a list of typed arrows.
  • Accessibility: colour-blind-safe palette, shape plus colour in the share grid, light and dark themes, reduced-motion respected.

4 · Validation & feedback

How strict

The arrows must be valid pushing (a real source with electrons, a sink that can accept them, balanced bookkeeping) and the result must match the target state of a step leaving the current state (up to symmetry and resonance). A correct product reached with nonsense arrows does not pass.

Decided 2026-11-24 (schema pass, details in schema-design.html):

  • Acceptance is by result, checked in the browser. Legal, non-redundant arrows whose result matches a target state are correct, so nothing is listed for acceptance. Equivalent atoms (either water H, any α-H) and resonance forms (including the other Kekulé ring) are both handled by comparing σ skeletons. That is one quick pass, with no list of resonance forms and nothing precomputed. A result with more charged atoms than the target counts as partial progress. The next step continues from the curated drawing.
  • A mechanism is a graph of states (revised 2026-11-24, replacing “no branching in v1”). Each step goes from one state to another and is preferred (the textbook route, used for hints and the reveal) or accepted (also correct). Correct routes may branch and must rejoin at a single product. The graph has no loops, and there is exactly one all-preferred route. Revised 2026-11-26 (item 10e): an accepted route may instead end at an alternative product (a minor regioisomer, e.g. ortho beside para); the all-preferred route ends at the main product. Reaching it is full marks; the step's explanation and the product's caption say what was made (the linter warns without a caption). Only regioisomers for now. Example: the Wittig, concerted [2+2] or stepwise via the betaine. A valid route the reaction doesn’t accept (e.g. O-attack) is a plausible misconception.
  • partialArrowSets (optional, per step) lists other complete arrow sets for that step, only so that “right so far” is recognised for them. It replaces the earlier “alternatives”.
  • Fish-hooks need full bookkeeping: H-abstraction takes 3 fish-hooks. The 2-arrow shorthand is not accepted and shows as partial progress.

Feedback layers

Precedence (decided 2026-11-24): when an authored misconception matches, it beats a generic rule. Only “meaningless arrow” errors, such as an empty source, come first.

Also decided 2026-11-24: partial progress comes before the generic chemistry rules, and shows both parts: the progress (“right so far, 1 more arrow” or the authored message) and any rule the incomplete arrows break (“… but carbon would have 10 electrons”). Full order: correct → meaningless arrows → misconceptions → partial progress → generic rules → wrong.

  1. Generic rules in code: octet violation, charge imbalance, reversed flow, empty source, sink that can’t accept electrons, wrong arrow type (pair vs fish-hook).
  2. Per-state misconception entries written in the content, each with a severity: wrong or plausible (valid but not the favoured pathway).
  3. Tiered hints: conceptual → narrowing → reveal (ghost arrows). Partial-progress messages (“right so far, N more arrows”) are also supported.

Chemistry conventions (v1)

  • Hydrogens that take part in a step are drawn explicitly in that step; the rest stay implicit.
  • Stereo (wedge and dash) is stored and displayed, not validated. The validator checks connectivity, formal charges and lone-pair/radical counts. SN2 inversion is an explanatory note. Stereo validation (CIP) is a v2 feature.
  • Reagents and conditions are labels over the reaction arrow, not drawable species.
  • Counterions (Na⁺, MgBr⁺) are drawn faded and not validated as participants.

6 · Community content & moderation

  • Contributors sign in with OAuth (e.g. GitHub or Google). Learners don’t need accounts. (8c: you only, by GitHub; contributors in 8e.)
  • Submission is gated on the linter passing.
  • Status flow: draft → submitted → changes requested | published. Only you (later, named reviewers) publish.
  • Published versions are immutable; an edit creates a new version, and old versions stay reachable because learner progress points at a version.
  • Review = a play-through plus a diff against the previous version.
  • Licence: CC BY-SA 4.0, with the contributor’s name on the reaction page, agreed at submission.
  • Contributors (item 8e, decided 2026-11-26):
    • Anyone who signs in is a contributor: they draft and submit; nothing goes live without a review.
    • Sign-in with GitHub or Google (you set up a Google sign-in app).
    • Reviewers: you, and the contributors you promote on a small Users page. Reviewers review and publish; only you change roles.
    • Improving someone else's published reaction is allowed: the copy goes to review as its next version, credited to both authors, without asking the original author first.
    • Agreed from the mockup (review.html): a sign-in page with GitHub and Google; Create gains Your mechanisms · Review (n) · Users; contributors see the reviewer's notes with “Go there →” and Submit for review; a review queue oldest first; a review screen with Play as learner, only what changed by default, notes and Publish or Request changes; a Users page for you with Contributor / Reviewer / Blocked; notifications in the site only for now; at most 20 open drafts per user (raised from 5 on 2026-11-26, so an assistant’s batch fits at once); editing a submitted draft takes it out of review; notes clear on resubmitting; nobody reviews their own submission (you can still publish your own directly).
    • Defaults chosen while building it (2026-11-26; say if you want any changed):
      • Only you publish directly; a reviewer's own drafts go through review like anyone's, and their own submissions are not in their queue.
      • Your GitHub account (the allow-list) makes its user the owner; a Google account joins a GitHub one only when both confirm the same email.
      • Your mechanisms can be filtered (2026-11-26): All · Drafts · Published, each with its count; Drafts are everything not yet published (draft, in review, changes requested).
      • Your mechanisms lists what you wrote and your own published reactions; other people's published reactions appear only under “Improve a published one” (“Suggest version n”).
      • Someone improving a reaction is added to its authors once. Saving the same content again keeps a submission in review; only a real change takes it out.
      • “What changed” lists each item separately (each hint, each wrong answer, each step's arrows and texts, each structure, the reaction's details), text with deletions and insertions word by word; a new wrong answer is drawn with its arrows when they are exact.

Drafting with an assistant decided 2026-11-26

To speed up content, an assistant drafts reactions and you only review them (and correct conditions) in Create before publishing.

  • Phase 1 (build item 10): the assistant (Claude Code in this repository) writes reaction files locally, lints them and pushes them to create.arrowpushing.com as drafts owned by your account.
    • Personal access tokens: stored hashed, revocable, expiring; made by a server command (create-token --user <email>). No token page in Create until phase 2.
    • A token may only read its user's reactions and save drafts: never submit, review, publish, start a next version or change roles. Publishing stays your click in Create.
    • Draft sync: check (the linter), push-draft (refuses a file with lint errors; refused by the server if the draft changed since the assistant last saw it, or is published), pull-draft (fetches your edited draft to work on). Your edits in Create are never silently overwritten.
    • The assistant writes every learner text (prompt, hints, wrong-answer and partial messages, explanations, summary), the textbook conditions (you correct them), tiers, daily eligibility and the track file. You are the only author for now; no “drafted with an assistant” mark.
  • Phase 2 (after the SEAr batch; details later): a remote MCP server on the same Render service (/mcp), same tokens, so other people's agents can draft too: format brief, check, apply arrows, pull, save draft; no publish tool; rate-limited; a token page in Create.
  • Reused: the design of RetrosynthWeb's refinement_mcp (checking separate from saving, no confirming tool, an account's token, rate limits), not its Python code. Not used: synkit's mechsearch search (weak on SEAr, heavy Python stack). Its arrow notation matches ours, so its reviewed routes could be imported one day.

8 · Process

  • Your global protocol is strict TDD: plan (stubs and test cases) → stubs and tests (Vitest) → red run → minimal implementation → refactor, and no “done” until 0 failures. It applies to the real build (validator, schema, linter), not the throwaway sketches.
  • Naming standard (2026-11-24): every name must be unambiguous to someone new to that part of the code. The reference level is eyring.py in aberki1234/eyring-uncertainty and RetrosynthWeb's transform_proposals. The rules are in the project CLAUDE.md, and terms are fixed in schema-design §0.
  • Lint and complexity checks (2026-11-24), the same as RetrosynthWeb's:
    • at most 6 parameters, at most 40 statements and complexity at most 12;
    • functions of at most 50 lines and files of at most 500 lines;
    • import layers with no cycles.
